Learn with purpose

Study methods.

Studying is more than rereading. Use an active strategy to stay curious, check what you remember, and make your time count.

Having trouble studying? Try one of these study methods during your next study session.

⌕

The SQ3R method

A five step way to read actively and turn a chapter into questions you can answer.

1

Survey
Skim through the reading and take note of headlines, subheadings, images, and charts.

2

Question
Come up with questions about the topic like, what is this chapter about? What do I already know?

3

Read
Read a section at a time and look for answers to your questions.

4

Recite
Summarize the key points in your own words, and answer the questions you wrote down earlier.

5

Review
Revisit your notes and then quiz yourself on the key points of the reading.

Source: University of St. Augustine for Health Sciences ↗
↟

Move to reset your focus

A short bit of movement can be a helpful transition into study time—or a way to reset when your focus dips.

Try a tiny reset

  1. Step away from your desk for 5–10 minutes.
  2. Take a walk, stretch, or get a glass of water.
  3. When you return, choose one small task to start.
Never underestimate a break. Try to avoid scrolling to keep your mind focused.
Source: University of St. Augustine for Health Sciences ↗

Note taking methods.

Keeping notes organized isn't always easy. Make it easy with these methods so you can review easier, later.

Try one approach for a week. Use the format that helps you find key ideas quickly when it's time to study.

◈

Color coded notes

Use a small, consistent color system to help important ideas stand out.

  • Red: key points or concepts to remember.
  • Yellow: highlight essential details.
  • One color per topic: use the same color for related themes.
  • Keep it selective: only color key information.
Start with two colors. If everything is highlighted, nothing stands out.
Source: University of St. Augustine for Health Sciences ↗
▤

The Cornell method

Divide your page into three areas so your notes naturally become a study guide.

1

Notes area
Capture lecture ideas, examples, and explanations as you listen.

2

Cue column
Add questions, main ideas, or vocabulary beside your notes.

3

Summary
Write a brief recap at the bottom in your own words.

Cover the notes column and use your questions to recall the ideas. Then check what you missed.
Source: Connections Academy ↗
Make starting easier

Planning methods.

Big tasks can feel less overwhelming when you decide what “done for now” looks like and plan breaks on purpose.

You don't have to wait for motivation to arrive. Pick one small next step, give it a little time, and begin.

◷

The Pomodoro technique

Work in a focused interval, then pause before starting another round. A common starting point is 25 minutes of work followed by a 5 minute break.

Make a round work for you

  1. Choose one clear goal for the next 25 minutes.
  2. Set a timer and work on just that task.
  3. Take a 5 minute break when the timer ends.
  4. After a few rounds, take a longer break.
During breaks, try a stretch, snack, or walk. If 25 minutes feels too long, shorten the work interval and build up gradually.
Source: Providence Ready ↗
↘

Break tasks into steps

Turn a large paper or project into small actions that are clear enough to start.

Instead of “work on my paper”

  • Choose a topic and write one question.
  • Find two useful sources.
  • Make a rough outline with three main points.
  • Write one paragraph, then take a break.
A good next step is specific and small enough to finish in one sitting. Celebrate progress, then decide the next step.
Source: University of Europe for Applied Sciences ↗
